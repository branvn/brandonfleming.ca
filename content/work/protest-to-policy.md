Road crashes are the leading killer of children and youth worldwide. Yet we
barely register it. Our language around it gives it away: we call them
'accidents' as though they were no one's decision. Somewhere along the way the
traffic fatality shifted from a moral wrong to a necessary evil.

In the 1970s the Netherlands was facing a struggle which modern day North
America is all too familiar with; high vehicle-related fatalities.
Specifically, it was children falling victim to these deadly incidents. This is
the story of how the Dutch rejected car-centricity through the perfect storm of
grassroots organization, institutional receptiveness, and economic catalyst,
and what it could mean for North America today.

## What I found

### Public outcry had to meet open-minded politicians

The Provos were clashing against car dominance from the mid 1960s and gained
public backing doing so, but agitation on its own doesn't build bike lanes.
Political actors started to notice the problem when grassroots activist groups
like "Stop the Child Murder" made traffic fatalities much more visible. Their
shocking demonstrations made the news and showed the country how bad things had
really gotten. This led to politicians hiring young engineers to make the roads
safer. Their solution? Adopt the approach citizens and activists had already
devised for this issue. Thus, the Dutch engineers began co-designing
transportation infrastructure with the citizens.

### The oil crisis provided a different kind of push

The oil crisis of 1973 created a sudden, sharp drop in fuel available to the
Netherlands. This meant many were without personal vehicles and fuel was
rationed. People got a taste of what it might be like to walk or bike the
streets without traffic. The tenuous future of oil loomed in front of the
politicians as they became further persuaded to restrict the car from more and
more roads.

## What can be gleaned from the Dutch mobility shift?

The lesson here isn't "just hold more protests." It's that a durable mobility
shift requires multiple factors lining up alongside an open policy window. In
the Dutch scenario, it was public pressure, institutional receptiveness, and an
economic shock which made the status quo untenable. Metro Vancouver possesses
much of that same bike activism appetite, but remains hamstrung by a fragmented
municipal landscape where bike lanes and sidewalks dissolve at municipal
boundaries. We cannot count on a 1970s oil crisis or Dutch unity (via the polder
model) to build us a safe sustainable future. What we can adopt is their most
critical realization: that traffic violence is not an inevitable "accident," but
a deliberate design choice. Until our regional leaders unite across municipal
borders, sustainable transit networks will remain fragmented.
